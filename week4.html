SESSION FOUR Truth, Assumptions, and the Stories We Tell Ourselves Learning to Separate Facts From Interpretation Student Reading | Session Four Key Scriptures: Proverbs 18:17, John 7:24, 1 Thessalonians 5:21, Proverbs 14:15 Introduction We do not always see situations as clearly as we believe. A conversation, a message, someone’s facial expression, or an unexpected decision can leave us forming conclusions before we have all the information. Our minds naturally attempt to explain what happens around us. Sometimes those explanations are accurate. Other times, assumptions become conclusions, and conclusions begin influencing our relationships. Discernment requires us to recognize the difference between what we know, what we believe, and what we still need to understand. Am I responding to the truth of a situation, or to the story I have created about the situation? 1. The First Explanation Is Not Always the Complete Truth Proverbs 18:17 The first person to explain a situation may sound convincing until additional information becomes available. This principle applies to everyday relationships. Someone may describe a disagreement from their perspective, leaving out details they do not know, misunderstand, or remember differently. Our own perspective can carry the same limitations. Consider receiving a message without a response. The fact is that no response has arrived. The assumption might be that the person is angry, ignoring us, or intentionally avoiding the conversation. Those possibilities may exist, but they are not established facts. Discernment allows us to recognize when we have moved beyond what we actually know. 2. Examine Before Making Conclusions John 7:24 Judging a situation requires more than reacting to appearances. We need understanding, patience, and a willingness to consider information beyond our initial impression. Someone’s tone may sound harsh because they are frustrated. Their silence may reflect exhaustion. Their decision may involve circumstances we have never considered. Recognizing these possibilities does not mean excusing harmful behavior. It means refusing to make conclusions without sufficient understanding. When something concerns us, we can ask questions, seek clarification, and evaluate the information honestly. 3. Test What You Hear and Believe 1 Thessalonians 5:21; Proverbs 14:15 Discernment requires examining information rather than accepting every explanation immediately. We may hear something about another person, receive advice, encounter an opinion, or interpret an experience. Before allowing that information to influence our decisions, we should consider whether the information is reliable, complete, and consistent with truth. Ask yourself: * What do I actually know? * What have I assumed? * Have I heard this directly or through someone else? * What information might be missing? * Have I allowed my emotions to influence my conclusion? A discerning man remains willing to reconsider his understanding when additional information becomes available. Personal Reflection * Have I recently made a conclusion without knowing the complete situation? * Do I tend to assume the worst when someone disappoints or frustrates me? * Have I repeated information without verifying whether the information was accurate? * What would change in my relationships if I became more careful with my assumptions? Session Four Accountability Facts Versus Assumptions Today, identify one situation you have been interpreting or thinking about. Write down: What I know: The facts I can confirm. What I assumed: The explanations or conclusions I created. What I do not know: Information that remains unclear. My next wise response: What I can do without allowing assumptions to control my actions. When appropriate, seek clarification directly and respectfully rather than allowing an assumption to become resentment. Come prepared to share what you learned about the way you interpret situations.
